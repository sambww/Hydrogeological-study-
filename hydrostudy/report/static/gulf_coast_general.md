The Gulf Coast Aquifer System is one of the major aquifers of Texas as designated by the Texas Water Development Board (TWDB). It underlies the coastal plain in a band that extends from the Louisiana border to the Rio Grande and consists of a thick wedge of unconsolidated to poorly consolidated sand, silt, clay and gravel that dips and thickens toward the Gulf of Mexico. Several stratigraphic frameworks have been proposed for these sediments; the hydrogeologic classification of Baker (1979), which subdivides the system on the basis of electric-log character, fauna, depositional facies and hydraulic properties, is the one adopted by the TWDB and by the District. From youngest to oldest the units are the Chicot Aquifer, the Evangeline Aquifer, the Burkeville confining unit, the Jasper Aquifer and the Catahoula confining unit.

The Chicot Aquifer comprises the Holocene alluvium, the Pleistocene Beaumont and Lissie formations and the Pliocene Willis Sand. Its sediments are interbedded sand, clay and gravel; because they are younger and less compacted, they are generally more permeable than the sediments of the underlying units. The Evangeline Aquifer is formed by the Goliad Sand and the upper part of the Fleming Formation. The Chicot and Evangeline sediments are lithologically similar and the contact between them is gradational; in practice the two units are distinguished by their electric-log signature and by differences in hydraulic conductivity, with Evangeline sands being more cemented and less permeable.

The Burkeville confining unit is the clay- and silt-dominated upper part of the Fleming Formation (Lagarto Clay) that separates the Evangeline Aquifer from the underlying Jasper Aquifer and restricts vertical flow between them. The Jasper Aquifer consists of the lower Lagarto, the Oakville Sandstone and, where they are sufficiently sandy, the upper beds of the Catahoula Formation; it is composed of land-derived sand and clay of Miocene age. The Catahoula confining unit, at the base of the system, is made up of tuffaceous and pyroclastic sand and clay of Oligocene to Miocene age that transmit little water regionally, although locally sandy intervals of the Catahoula are developed for supply.

Recharge to the system occurs where each unit crops out in the updip parts of the coastal plain, principally by infiltration of precipitation; downdip the units are confined by their own clay beds and by the overlying units. Regional groundwater flow is from the outcrop areas toward the coast and toward the major stream valleys, modified locally by pumping.
